Reinforcement learning for LLM training, from zero

One equation, three ways to fill it in

PPO, GRPO and SPO train a language model the same way. Let it answer, score the answer, then make good answers more likely. They differ in one detail: how each decides what counts as better than expected. This page builds that idea one step at a time, using a single arithmetic prompt as the running example.

\[ \nabla_\theta J \;=\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\; \adv{A}\cdot\nabla_\theta \log \pol{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)} \;\Big] \qquad\text{with}\qquad \adv{A} \;=\; \rew{r} \;-\; \bas{b} \]
  • πpolicy: the model's probabilities
  • rreward: the grader's score
  • bbaseline: the score you expected
  • Aadvantage: reward minus baseline
PPOThe baseline is predicted by a second neural network, the critic.
GRPOThe baseline is the average score of several answers to the same prompt.
SPOThe baseline is a running record of how often the model solved this prompt before.

If the equation means nothing yet, that is expected. By the end of step 3 you will be able to read every symbol in it. The colors stay the same all the way down the page.

Step 1

The RL words, in LLM words

Reinforcement learning has its own vocabulary, but for LLM training each word maps to something you already know. One round of training looks like this:

Promptx→ Modelπθ→ Responsey→ Graderchecks the answer→ Rewardr→ Update weightsθ
Policy πθ
The LLM itself. θ (theta) is the full set of its weights.
State
The prompt plus the tokens written so far.
Action
The next token.
Trajectory
One complete response. Also called a rollout or an episode.
Reward r
A number from a grader. In our example: 1 if the final answer is 408, otherwise 0.
Objective J(θ)
The average reward the model earns. Training tries to raise it.

How to read πθ(y | x)

It is the probability that the model with weights θ writes response y when given prompt x. The bar means "given". A response is a chain of tokens, and the model picks one token at a time, so the probability of the whole response is the token probabilities multiplied together:

The0.90× answer0.85× is0.95× 4080.20= whole response0.145

Papers almost always write the logarithm of this number. Logs turn the product into a sum, which is easier for both computers and calculus:

\[ \log \pol{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)} \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pol{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,\, y_{\lt t})} \;=\; -0.105 - 0.163 - 0.051 - 1.609 \;=\; -1.93 \]

Here \(y_t\) is token number t and \(y_{\lt t}\) means all tokens before it. This sum is the same quantity the cross-entropy loss computes in ordinary fine-tuning. Keep that in mind, because the next step depends on it.

Step 2

Policy gradient: make rewarded answers more likely

The whole method fits in one sentence. Sample a response, score it, and nudge the weights so that response becomes more likely in proportion to its score. Written as math:

\[ \nabla_\theta J \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\,y\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\; \rew{r(y)}\;\nabla_\theta \log \pol{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)} \;\Big] \]

Read it from right to left.

  • \(\nabla_\theta \log \pol{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}\) is a direction. It says how to change every weight so that this exact response becomes more likely. Supervised fine-tuning follows the same direction when y is a human-written target.
  • \(\rew{r(y)}\) says how hard to push. Reward 1 means a full push. Reward 0 means no push at all.
  • \(\mathbb{E}_{\,y\sim\pi_\theta}[\,\cdot\,]\) means "average over many responses sampled from the model". In practice you sample a batch and take the mean.
  • \(\nabla_\theta J\) is the result: the direction that raises average reward.

So this kind of RL is fine-tuning on the model's own samples, with each sample weighted by its reward. In code the two losses differ by one factor:

# supervised fine-tuning: imitate a human answer
loss = -logprob(y_human)

# policy gradient (REINFORCE): imitate your own answers, weighted by reward
loss = -reward * logprob(y_sampled)

To watch it work, shrink the LLM down to a model that can only give four answers to one prompt. When one answer is pushed up, the other three go down to make room, because probabilities sum to 1.

Try this. With right-or-wrong rewards, sample a few times. Only a sampled 408 changes anything, and wrong answers are ignored. Then switch to partial credit and sample until you draw 398. It earns 0.6, so it gets pushed up even though it is wrong. That flaw is what the next step fixes.

Step 3

Baseline and advantage: compare with what you expected

With partial credit every answer earns something, so every sampled answer is pushed up. The model still improves on average, because better answers are pushed harder. But each single update is noisy, and a run of unlucky samples can lock in a wrong answer. The fix is to subtract the reward you expected before looking:

\[ \adv{A} \;=\; \rew{r} \;-\; \bas{b} \]
  • b is the baseline, the reward you expected.
  • A is the advantage, the surprise. Positive means better than expected, so push the response up. Negative means worse than expected, so push it down. Zero means nothing to learn.

Replace r with A in the policy gradient and you have the equation from the top of the page. Subtracting a baseline does not change where training heads on average. It only removes noise, as long as b is chosen without looking at which response was sampled.

Why subtracting b changes nothing on average

The extra term is the baseline times the average nudge direction, and the average nudge direction is zero. Probabilities always sum to 1, so pushing them all "up" by their own share cancels out:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\bas{b}\,\nabla_\theta\log\pol{\pi_\theta(y)}\big] = \bas{b}\sum_y \pol{\pi_\theta(y)}\,\frac{\nabla_\theta \pol{\pi_\theta(y)}}{\pol{\pi_\theta(y)}} = \bas{b}\,\nabla_\theta \sum_y \pol{\pi_\theta(y)} = \bas{b}\,\nabla_\theta 1 = 0 \]

A good baseline is therefore worth a lot. For an LLM it is also hard to get, because the expected reward is different for every prompt. An easy prompt might be solved 95% of the time and a hard one 5%. PPO, GRPO and SPO are three answers to one question: where does b come from?

Step 4

PPO: learn the baseline with a second network

Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) is the classic method, and the one used in the original RLHF recipe for instruction-following models. It adds two ideas to the policy gradient.

Idea 1: a critic predicts the reward

Train a second network, the critic \(\bas{V_\phi(s)}\). It reads the prompt and the tokens written so far, and predicts the final reward. It is trained by plain regression against the rewards that were actually observed. Because the critic makes a prediction at every token, the advantage can be different at every token: it is how much the prediction moved when that token was written.

The one-step surprise

The advantage in Figure 4 has a name and a formula. Papers call it the TD error and write it δ (delta). It is the change in the critic's prediction caused by writing one token:

\[ \delta_t \;=\; \underbrace{\rew{r_t} + \gamma\,\bas{V_\phi(s_{t+1})}}_{\text{what the critic thinks now}} \;-\; \underbrace{\bas{V_\phi(s_t)}}_{\text{what it thought before}} \]
  • \(\bas{V_\phi(s_t)}\) is the critic's prediction of the final reward just before token t is written.
  • \(\bas{V_\phi(s_{t+1})}\) is its prediction just after.
  • \(\rew{r_t}\) is any reward handed out at this exact step. In LLM tasks it is 0 for every token except the last. After the last token there is nothing left to predict, so the prediction counts as 0 and the real reward takes its place.
  • \(\gamma\) (gamma) is a discount on future reward. For LLM training it is 1 or very close to 1, and it is 1 in the figures here.

Think of the win-probability bar in a chess broadcast. Before a move it reads 60%, and after the move it reads 10%. Only that move happened in between, so the move was worth −50 points. That drop is δ. The surprises of a whole response always add up to the final reward minus the first prediction, so δ splits the total surprise across the tokens.

GAE: also count the surprises that follow

If the critic were perfect, δ alone would be the advantage. A real critic is often wrong or slow. It may miss a mistake at the token where it happens and only react a few tokens later. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) therefore gives each token its own surprise plus a fading share of the later ones:

\[ \adv{A_t} \;=\; \delta_t \;+\; (\gamma\lambda)\,\delta_{t+1} \;+\; (\gamma\lambda)^2\,\delta_{t+2} \;+\; \dots \]

λ (lambda) is a number between 0 and 1 that sets how fast the share fades.

  • λ = 0 trusts the critic fully and uses only the token's own surprise. That is precise when the critic is good and wrong when the critic is wrong.
  • λ = 1 counts every later surprise in full. The advantage becomes the final reward minus the prediction before the token, so it no longer depends on the critic's later predictions. The price is noise: a token is blamed or credited for everything that came after it.
  • In practice λ sits in between, and 0.95 is a common choice.

In code it is one backward pass over the tokens:

# last token: only its own surprise
A[T] = delta[T]
# every earlier token, going backward
A[t] = delta[t] + gamma * lam * A[t+1]

Idea 2: clip, so one batch can be reused safely

Generating responses is the expensive part, so you want several gradient steps from each batch. After the first step the model has changed, though, and the batch was sampled from the old model. PPO tracks how far the model has moved on each token with a ratio:

\[ \ratio_t \;=\; \frac{\pol{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)}}{\pol{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid s_t)}} \]

A ratio of 1 means the model is unchanged on this token. A ratio of 1.3 means the new model is 30% more likely to write it. Papers call this \(r_t(\theta)\), which is easy to confuse with reward, so this page writes "ratio". The objective to maximize is:

\[ L^{\text{clip}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\,\min\big(\;\ratio_t\,\adv{A_t}\,,\;\; \operatorname{clip}(\ratio_t,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\adv{A_t}\;\big)\Big] \]
  • \(\ratio_t\,\adv{A_t}\) is the policy gradient from step 3 in disguise. Raising the probability of a good token raises it.
  • \(\operatorname{clip}(\cdot)\) cuts the ratio off outside the range from 1 − ε to 1 + ε. ε is usually 0.2.
  • \(\min\) keeps the more pessimistic of the two. The effect: once a token has moved far enough in the helpful direction, it stops earning credit. If it moved in the harmful direction, the full penalty still applies.

What it costs for LLMs. The critic is another network of similar size, so it takes extra memory and compute. It is also hard to train well, because rewards usually arrive only at the end of a response and every prompt is a different problem. A poor critic gives a poor baseline. GRPO removes the critic for this reason.

Step 5

GRPO: let a group of answers be the baseline

Group Relative Policy Optimization comes from DeepSeek (introduced in DeepSeekMath, 2024, and used to train DeepSeek-R1). It drops the critic. For each prompt, sample a group of G responses and grade them all. The group's average reward is the baseline:

\[ \adv{\hat A_i} \;=\; \frac{\rew{r_i} \;-\; \bas{\operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)} \]

The numerator is the same r − b as before. Dividing by the standard deviation puts easy and hard prompts on the same scale. Worked example with 8 responses, 2 of them correct: the mean is 0.25 and the std is 0.43. A correct response gets (1 − 0.25) / 0.43 = +1.73. A wrong one gets (0 − 0.25) / 0.43 = −0.58.

Every token of response i then receives that one number, and it goes into the same clipped objective as PPO:

\[ J_{\text{GRPO}} = \mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\min\Big(\ratio_{i,t}\,\adv{\hat A_i},\; \operatorname{clip}(\ratio_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,\adv{\hat A_i}\Big)\Bigg] - \beta\, D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pol{\pi_\theta}\,\|\,\pol{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\big) \]

Read it from the outside in: for each of the G responses, for each of its tokens, take PPO's clipped term, then average. The last term penalizes drifting far from a reference model, and many later recipes set β to 0.

The weak spot: groups that all agree

If all G responses are correct, or all are wrong, the rewards are identical. Every advantage is zero and the group teaches nothing, although you paid to generate it. For a model that solves the prompt with probability p, the chance of that happening is \(p^G + (1-p)^G\).

Two more costs. A group can only be scored when its slowest response has finished, which hurts when responses involve long tool calls. And like SPO below, GRPO gives one number to a whole response, so it cannot point at the token where things went wrong the way a critic can.

Step 6

SPO: remember how each prompt went last time

Single-stream Policy Optimization (Xu and Ding, 2025) goes back to one response per prompt, as in classic RL. The baseline comes from memory. For every prompt in the training set, SPO keeps a small running record of how often the model has solved it. It has three parts.

Part 1: a value tracker per prompt

For each prompt x, keep two fading counters: α for successes and β for failures. The baseline is the success rate they imply:

\[ \bas{\hat v(x)} \;=\; \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta} \]

Each time the prompt is visited, the model writes one response and earns a reward of 1 or 0. First shrink both counters by a factor ρ, then add the new result:

\[ \alpha \;\leftarrow\; \rho\,\alpha + \rew{r} \qquad\qquad \beta \;\leftarrow\; \rho\,\beta + (1-\rew{r}) \]

ρ (rho) is a forgetting factor between 0 and 1. Old evidence is multiplied by ρ on every visit, so it fades, and the tracker remembers roughly the last 1 / (1 − ρ) visits. The advantage uses the baseline from before the new response was seen, which keeps it fair in the sense of step 3:

\[ \adv{A} \;=\; \rew{r} \;-\; \bas{\hat v_{\text{before}}(x)} \]

How fast should it forget? If the model has changed a lot since it last saw this prompt, old results say little about the current model. SPO measures that change with the KL divergence D between the current policy and the policy at the last visit, and sets

\[ \rho \;=\; 2^{-D / D_{\text{half}}} \quad\text{kept within } [\rho_{\min},\,\rho_{\max}] = [0.875,\; 0.96] \]

A big change gives a small ρ and fast forgetting. In the figure below you set ρ by hand to feel what it does.

Part 2: normalize across the whole batch

GRPO rescales advantages inside each group. SPO has no groups, so it rescales across the whole batch \(\mathcal{B}\) of different prompts:

\[ \adv{\tilde A} \;=\; \frac{\adv{A} - \mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}} \]

\(\mu_{\mathcal{B}}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}\) are the mean and standard deviation of all advantages in the batch. With thousands of samples behind them, these statistics are far steadier than the ones from a group of 8. The result goes into the same PPO clipped objective, one value for every token of the response.

Part 3: spend samples where there is something to learn

A prompt the model always solves, or never solves, gives advantages near zero. SPO uses the tracker to choose which prompts to visit, with weight

\[ w(x) \;\propto\; \sqrt{\bas{\hat v(x)}\,\big(1-\bas{\hat v(x)}\big)} \;+\; \epsilon \qquad (\epsilon = 0.05) \]

The square root term is largest for prompts the model solves about half the time. The small ε keeps every prompt in rotation. This is how SPO avoids the wasted compute in Figure 8.

What it costs. The tracker is a table with one entry per prompt, so SPO needs a training set whose prompts come back many times. The base form assumes right-or-wrong rewards. It also needs a warm-up pass to fill the table, 8 responses per prompt in the paper. In return, the authors report a 3.4 point average gain over GRPO on math benchmarks with Qwen3-8B (scored by majority vote over 32 samples), and a 4.35× throughput gain in a simulated agentic setting where response times vary widely.

If you remember three things

  1. Policy gradient is fine-tuning on the model's own samples, weighted by advantage.
  2. Advantage is reward minus a baseline, and a better baseline means less noise.
  3. PPO learns the baseline, GRPO averages it from a group, and SPO remembers it per prompt.
